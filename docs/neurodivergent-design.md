# Neurodivergent Design Principles

## Design goal

Do not force a wandering, associative, interrupted, or overloaded mind into a rigid filing system. Give every thought somewhere safe to land and make returning easy.

Neurodivergent people are not one audience with one need. These principles are starting hypotheses to test with people who have different access needs, tools, cultures, and working styles.

## The low-friction loop

### 1. Capture

Ask only:

- What is this?
- Where did it come from?

Offer **Save without processing**. Never make filing, interpretation, or completeness a condition of preservation.

### 2. Clarify

Present one decision at a time. Suggested order:

1. creator or author;
2. original date;
3. source location;
4. claim type;
5. relationship;
6. rights, consent, and affiliation.

Every step needs `unknown`, `not applicable`, and `return later` where appropriate.

### 3. Connect

Let people record an association without declaring it factual. Ask:

> How is this connected?

Then:

> What supports that connection?

Keep the relationship label and its evidence together.

### 4. Pause

Create a checkpoint automatically:

- what was preserved;
- what was confirmed;
- what remains unresolved;
- the last completed action;
- one recommended next action.

### 5. Resume

Open at the checkpoint, not at a dashboard. Use plain language such as:

> Three sources are safely preserved. One authorship question remains. Continue there or capture something new.

## Interaction rules

- Prefer one primary action per screen or section.
- Show progress as orientation, not pressure.
- Avoid streaks, countdowns, red failure states, or shame-based completeness scores.
- Preserve work immediately and communicate when it is safe.
- Avoid surprise moves, renames, merges, deletions, publication, or sharing.
- Keep labels visible beside claims; do not rely on color alone.
- Support keyboard, touch, screen readers, zoom, reflow, high contrast, and reduced motion.
- Make destructive and external actions clearly distinct from local documentation.
- Explain errors with a recovery action and retain entered information.
- Use short summaries first, with details available on demand.
- Allow people to choose list, timeline, table, or relationship-map views without changing the underlying evidence.

## Language patterns

Prefer:

- “Safely preserved”
- “Not reviewed yet”
- “What would you like to confirm next?”
- “This is currently an interpretation.”
- “The available evidence does not establish that relationship.”
- “You can return later.”

Avoid:

- “Incomplete” when no immediate action is required
- “Invalid” for uncertainty or missing evidence
- “You forgot”
- “Finish everything”
- language that equates organization, speed, memory, or consistency with care or credibility

## Test scenarios

An implementation should be tested with people who:

- have only a phone or tablet;
- stop mid-task and return days later;
- capture many loosely related sources quickly;
- experience working-memory limits or decision fatigue;
- use assistive technology;
- need to preserve material before they can safely interpret it;
- change their understanding as new evidence appears.

Success means a person can preserve one source, understand its current status, stop without losing work, and know the next action when they return.
